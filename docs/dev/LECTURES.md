checked at high zoom. Everything the course material says is here; content authors work from this file, not from the

## L1020 · 10/20 · Chapter 5: Rings (cycloalkanes) · 3 pages

**Page 1. Cyclopropane.**
- Propane's C-C-C angle is 109.5°. Cyclopropane's internal angle is 60°: this is **angle strain**.
- Orbital picture: the sp³ orbitals cannot point along the lines between the carbons, so they overlap off-axis and make
  "**bent**" bonds. Bent bond = less overlap = **weaker bond**.
- C-C bond strength: propane 88 kcal/mol; cyclopropane 65 kcal/mol.
- Drawing of cyclopropane with all six H shown, then a Newman projection looking down C1-C2 (C3 bridges front and back
  carbons): all the C-H bonds are **eclipsed**, which is **torsional strain**: 6 C-H/C-H eclipsing interactions.
- Summary box: **Ring strain: 1. angle strain, 2. torsional strain.**

**Page 2. Cyclobutane and cyclopentane.**
- Butane 109.5°. Flat cyclobutane: 1. angle strain, 109.5° forced to 90°; 2. torsional strain, 8 eclipsing C-H/C-H
  interactions.
- Real cyclobutane adopts a "**puckered**" conformation (angle 88°): this **relieves some torsional strain**.
- Cyclopentane: pentane 109.5°, flat cyclopentane 108°: angle strain very little. Torsional strain: 10 C-H/C-H
  eclipsing interactions if flat.
- "2 energy minima conformations" are drawn (the envelope and the half-chair): **lots of torsional strain relieved, at
  the expense of angle strain**. (HW: build a model.)

**Page 3. Cyclohexane.**
- Flat cyclohexane would have 120° angles (angle strain, 109.5° to 120°) and 12 C-H/C-H eclipsing interactions.
- The "**chair**" conformation of cyclohexane: **no torsional strain, no angle strain** (C-C-C angle 111.5°).
- Chair drawn with all 12 H, in three colours: "lines of the same colour are parallel to each other" (each C-H bond is
  parallel to ring bonds one bond away). 1. All C's are tetrahedral. 2. All C-H bonds on adjacent carbons are staggered.

## L1022 · 10/22 · Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) · 3 pages

**Page 1.**
- Ethyl acetate is drawn as the running example. Other ways to characterize a compound: MS, boiling point etc., IR, GC-FID.
- Spectroscopy in general: light source (hν) → sample → detector. The sample **absorbs specific frequencies**, and
  those tell us the **structure**. Absorption lifts something from a lower energy state to a higher one; ΔE = energy of
  the hν absorbed.
- A sample of protons: each nucleus is a tiny magnet (N/S). With no field the magnets point in **random orientations**.
- The same protons in an **external magnetic field**: most are **aligned with** the field (lower energy), some are
  **aligned against** it (higher energy). The gap is ΔE = hν.

**Page 2.**
- Graph: ΔE grows as the strength of the external field (B₀) grows (two diverging lines from zero).
- Two kinds: ¹H NMR and ¹³C NMR.
- **Electrons have their own magnetic field**, which **counters the external field**: electrons "**shield**" the nucleus.
  The nucleus experiences a net field: **B₀ − Bₑ = net magnetic field** (external field minus the electrons' field).
- Fluoroethane (CH₃CH₂F) drawn with its H's: the CH₂ hydrogens next to F and the CH₃ hydrogens are in
  **different environments → different NMR signals**. (F pulls electron density away: less shielding near F.)

**Page 3.**
- The allyl cation and its resonance partner are drawn (the two end CH₂ groups are equivalent by resonance; the middle
  H is different).
- Fluoroethane: **¹³C NMR, how many signals?** Two (C1 and C2). **¹H NMR:** two signals (H₁: the three CH₃ hydrogens;
  H₂: the two CH₂ hydrogens).
- Bromoethene (vinyl bromide) drawn with H₁, H₂, H₃ labelled: all three hydrogens are different (three signals).
- The lecture stops at counting signals. The exam also uses splitting (a "septet"), the n + 1 rule: a proton with n
  equivalent neighbours on the next carbon appears as n + 1 lines (6 neighbours → septet, as in an isopropyl CH).

## L1029 · 10/29 · Chapter 6: acids, bases, organometallic reagents · 5 pages

**Page 1.**
- Functional groups: **amines** R-NH₂; **alcohols** R-OH; **ethers** R-O-R; **thiols** R-SH; **sulfides** R-S-R;
  **ammonium** R-NH₃⁺.
- Lewis acids and bases. Periodic-table sketch around C N O / P S: **basicity increases to the left** across a row and
  **increases going up** a column.
- HS⁻ < HO⁻ < H₂N⁻ < H₃C⁻: **increasing base strength**; H₃C⁻ is the **strongest base**.
- Their conjugate acids H₂S, H₂O, NH₃, CH₄: acid strength increases in the opposite direction (H₂S the strongest acid).
- **pKa values:** H-I −10; H-Br −9; H-Cl −8; H₃O⁺ −1.5; H-F 4; CF₃CO₂H −1.5; R-CO₂H 4; H₂O 15.7; R-OH ~16;
  RNH₂ 38; H-C≡C-H 25.

**Page 2.**
- R-H (alkane) 50 to 60.
- RCO₂H (pKa 4) versus CF₃CO₂H (pKa −1.5): the conjugate base CF₃CO₂⁻ is **stabilized by induction** (the three F
  pull electron density through the bonds): more stable base, stronger acid.
- **Factors that stabilize a base: Induction, Size, Hybridization, Aromaticity, Resonance, Electronegativity.**
- H-A ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ with equilibrium constant Ka; **pKa = −log Ka**.
- H-C≡C-H pKa 25 versus H₃C-CH₃ pKa 60: the acetylide carbon is **sp, more s character, more stable** anion; the ethyl
  anion is sp³.

**Page 3.**
- H₂S pKa 10 versus H₂O 15.8: in HS⁻ the **larger atom "spreads out" the charge → more stable**.
- Three acids in a row: H₂S 10; acetic acid 4; HBr −9; with conjugate bases HS⁻, acetate, Br⁻.
- **Solvent effects. Table 6.8, p. 247.** pKa in solution: H₂O 15.7; CH₃OH 15.5 (**most acidic**); CH₃CH₂OH 15.9;
  (CH₃)₂CHOH 16.5; (CH₃)₃COH 17.0 (**least acidic**). In the **gas phase the relative acidity runs the other way**
  (acidity increases down this list: tert-butanol is the most acidic).

**Page 4.**
- Why: CH₃O⁻ is surrounded by many solvent molecules (**more solvation**); (CH₃)₃CO⁻ has a **larger nonpolar group,
  fewer solvent molecules around** it (**less solvation**). **Solvation → stabilization.**
- Equilibria: H₂O (15.7) + ⁻OH ⇌ HO⁻ + H-OH (15.7): **K = 1**.
  H₂O (15.7) + ⁻NH₂ → HO⁻ + NH₃ (38): lies to the right.
  NH₃ (38) + ⁻CH₃ → ⁻NH₂ + CH₄ (50 to 60): lies to the right.
- CH₄ → ⁻CH₃? A **very strong base**. Li-CH₃: a **strong base**.

**Page 5. Organometallic reagents.**
- **1) Organolithium reagents, R-Li** (R = hydrocarbon). The C-Li bond is polarized δ⁻ on C, δ⁺ on Li (think
  H₃C⁻ Li⁺); compare H₃C-Cl, polarized the other way (δ⁺ on C). Aside: HO-Li ⇌ HO⁻ Li⁺.
- Made by: **R-Br (or R-Cl) + Li⁰ (2 equivalents), in pentane → R-Li + LiBr.**
- **2) Grignard reagents, R-Mg-X** (X = halogen). Think R:⁻ (MgX)⁺ → a **strong base**.
- Made by: **R-X + Mg⁰ in diethyl ether → R-MgX.**

## L1103 · 11/03 · Organometallics finished; Chapter 7: substitution (SN2) · 4 pages

**Page 1.**
- RLi and RMgX both act as **R:⁻**. H₃C-Li is polarized δ⁻C/δ⁺Li (H₃C:⁻ Li⁺); H₃C-Br is δ⁺C/δ⁻Br and is **not ionic**.
- H₃C-Li (base) + H₂O (acid) ⇌ CH₄ + Li⁺ ⁻OH. Mechanism: the carbon lone pair takes H from water, the H-O electrons
  go to oxygen. pKa of water ~15.8, of methane ~50 to 60: these are **very strong bases**.
- **Synthesis (HW): starting material propene → product 1, propane with D on the middle carbon (2-deuteriopropane);
  product 2, propane. What steps are needed?** (Answer: add HBr to propene to make 2-bromopropane; Mg in ether, or
  2 Li, to make the organometallic; then D₂O gives product 1, H₂O gives product 2.)

**Page 2. Chapter 7.**
- Warm-up mechanism: HO⁻ (Lewis base) + H-Cl (Lewis acid; H is δ⁺) ⇌ HO-H + Cl⁻.
- **Substitution:** HO⁻ + H₃C-Cl ⇌ HO-CH₃ + Cl⁻. The carbon is δ⁺. HO⁻ = Lewis base = "**nucleophile**";
  CH₃Cl = Lewis acid = "**electrophile**"; Cl = **leaving group**. The three H's flip over: "**inversion**".
- HW: why does the reverse reaction not occur?
- With a stereocentre: (R)-CH₃CHD-Cl + HO⁻ → (S)-CH₃CHD-OH + Cl⁻: **one enantiomer gives one enantiomer: inversion.**

**Page 3.**
- Another example: HS⁻ + H₃C-I ⇌ H-S-CH₃ + I⁻. Rate data:

  | trial | [HS⁻] | [H₃C-I] | rate |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | A | 1 | 1 | 1 |
  | B | 2 | 1 | 2 |
  | C | 1 | 2 | 2 |
  | D | 2 | 2 | 4 |

  **rate = k[HS⁻][H₃C-I] → bimolecular substitution ⇒ SN2.**
- The rate-determining step involves **both the nucleophile and the electrophile**; so does the transition state.
- **S_N2: Substitution, Nucleophilic, bimolecular (2).**
- **1. Concerted reaction:** all at once, **no intermediate**. Energy diagram: one hump from start to product.

**Page 4.**
- **2. Inversion at the electrophilic atom.** Transition state: [Nu···C···LG] with R₁ up and R₂, R₃ on a dash and a
  wedge (the three groups in one plane, carbon five-coordinate); the product has the three groups flipped to the other
  side.

## L1105 · 11/05 · SN2 continued · 4 pages

**Page 1.**
- SN2: **1. second order, rate = k[Nu:⁻][E⁺]. 2. concerted, no intermediates. 3. inversion at the electrophile atom.**
- Example of inversion: 3-iodocyclohexanone with I on a wedge + NaSH (Na⁺ ⁻SH) → the SH is on a dash, + Na⁺ I⁻
  ("**Walden inversion**").
- A: I-CH₃ + I⁻ → H₃C-I + I⁻. B: I-CH₃ + ⁻OH → HOCH₃ + I⁻. C: I-CH₃ + (CH₃)₃CO⁻ → (CH₃)₃C-OCH₃ + I⁻.
  **HW: 1. sketch the transition state for each reaction. 2. consider Hammond's postulate. 3. consider the geometry of
  the transition state.**
- **1. Effect of electrophile structure on the SN2 rate** (LG = leaving group, R = hydrocarbon):
  methyl > primary > secondary >>> tertiary (**no SN2**). **Steric hindrance slows down the approaching Nu:⁻.**

**Page 2.**
- **2. Leaving-group effect on SN2.** Four ethyl compounds each with ⁻SH:
  CH₃CH₂-Br → CH₃CH₂-SH + Br⁻ (works). CH₃CH₂-OH → **N.R.**: HO⁻ is a **strong base, poor LG**.
  CH₃CH₂-F → no SN2: F⁻ is **not a good LG** (HF pKa ~4). CH₃CH₂-OTs → CH₃CH₂-SH + TsO⁻: tosylate is a **good LG**.
- **3. Effect of the nucleophile on SN2. Table 7.5, p. 295** (relative rate of SN2 reaction = a measure of
  **nucleophilicity**: how fast can it displace the LG):

  | Nu: | relative rate |
  |---|---|
  | HC≡C:⁻ | 126,000 |
  | HS⁻ | 126,000 |
  | I⁻ | 80,000 |
  | HO⁻ | 16,000 |
  | N₃⁻ | 8,000 |
  | :NH₃ | 8,000 |
  | Cl⁻ | 1,000 |
  | CH₃CO₂⁻ | 630 |
  | CH₃OH | 1 |
  | H₂O | 1 |

- "**Bulkier Nu: → less nucleophilic.**"

**Page 3.**
- tert-Butoxide, (CH₃)₃CO⁻: a **poor nucleophile**.
- Periodic-table sketch around C N O / S: **basicity increases to the left and up; nucleophilicity increases to the
  left and down.**
- **4. Effect of solvent on SN2.** Triethylamine + iodoethane → tetraethylammonium⁺ + I⁻.
  Reaction A: solvent 25:75 H₂O:ethanol. Reaction B: solvent 50:50 H₂O:ethanol (**more polar solvent**) → **faster**.
  Two energy curves: the more polar solvent **stabilizes the transition state more** (the T.S. has δ⁺ on N and δ⁻ on I,
  surrounded by solvent, while the starting materials are neutral): "T.S. is stabilized more than the starting
  materials → lowers Ea."

**Page 4.**
- The opposite case: HO⁻ + H₃C-S⁺(CH₃)₂ → HO-CH₃ + S(CH₃)₂, both reactants charged and solvated; in the T.S.
  [HO^δ−···CH₃···S^δ+] the charge is being destroyed.
  Reaction A: less polar solvent → **faster reaction**. Reaction B: more polar solvent → **stabilizes the starting
  materials more (greater solvation)**; it stabilizes the T.S. too, but to a lesser degree. Energy curves: the more
  polar solvent lowers the start more than the top, so the barrier is higher.

## L1112 · 11/12 · E2 · 4 pages

**Page 1.**
- (CH₃)₃C-Br + NaOH in H₂O: the alcohol is **not formed**; the product is 2-methylpropene. (With H₂O alone: SN1,
  the alcohol.) Rate data:

  | trial | [t-BuBr] | [⁻OH] | rate |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | A | 1 | 1 | 1 |
  | B | 2 | 1 | 2 |
  | C | 1 | 2 | 2 |
  | D | 2 | 2 | 4 |

  **rate = k[t-BuBr][⁻OH] → bimolecular elimination ⇒ E2.**
- Energy diagram: one hump; the T.S. contains both E⁺ and Nu:⁻ → **concerted**.
- Mechanism: HO⁻ takes the H on the carbon next to the C-Br carbon; the C-H electrons form the π bond; Br⁻ leaves.

**Page 2.**
- T.S. = [HO···H···C···C···Br] with partial bonds.
- **E2: 1. bimolecular. 2. concerted. 3. strong base.** (HO⁻ → strong base.)
- Example: (2S,3S)-2-bromo-3-methylpentane (drawn with wedges and dashes) + NaOCH₂CH₃, E2 → three alkenes:
  A, 3-methyl-1-pentene; B and C, the two geometric isomers of 3-methyl-2-pentene. **HW: which is the major E2
  product?** (Answer: put the C3-H anti to Br and eliminate: (E)-3-methyl-2-pentene, the isomer in which the two
  methyl groups are on the same side.)
- The T.S. for A is drawn, then a Newman projection and a sawhorse: the H being removed and the Br are on opposite
  sides, an **anti-periplanar relationship** → **optimal orbital overlap → more stable T.S. → faster**.

**Page 3.**
- Anti-periplanar: the C-H electrons push in from behind the C-Br bond: "electrons back-attack, like SN2".
  Syn-periplanar (H and Br on the same side): **orbital overlap is not optimal → less stable T.S.**
- **Predict the E2 product: 1. find the H's adjacent to the LG. 2. determine the anti-periplanar H's. 3. draw the
  conformation of the starting compound where H and LG are anti-periplanar. 4. perform the elimination → the major E2
  product comes from the anti-periplanar H.** "**The most important factor for E2.**"

**Page 4.**
- 2-hexyl-LG + NaOCH₃ in HOCH₃ → **Saytzeff** product 1, 2-hexene (**more substituted**) and **Hofmann** product 2,
  1-hexene (**less substituted**):

  | LG | 1 (Saytzeff) | 2 (Hofmann) |
  |---|---|---|
  | I | 81% | 19% |
  | Br | 72% | 28% |
  | Cl | 67% | 33% |
  | F | 30% | 70% |

- F is a "**Hofmann LG**" → an **E1cb-like E2** (with a poor leaving group the C-H bond breaks ahead of the C-LG bond;
  the transition state has carbanion character, which is better on the less substituted carbon).

## The decision flowchart (handout, 1 page)

"Flow Chart for Substitution and Elimination Reactions": R₃C-LG + Nu⁻.
- **Methyl?** → SN2. (A methyl carbocation is unstable and will never form, therefore no SN1 or E1; no β-hydrogen,
  therefore no E2.)
- **Primary?** Good Nu / weak base → SN2. Otherwise: bulky strong base → E2; non-bulky strong base → SN2.
  (For methyl and primary carbons, except benzylic and allylic, only E2 and SN2 are possible because of low
  carbocation stability.)
- **Secondary?** Polar protic solvent (solvates negative ions)? If yes: weak base / poor Nu → E1/SN1; otherwise strong
  base → E2. If no: weak base / fair Nu → SN2; otherwise strong base → E2.
- **Tertiary** (too much steric hindrance for SN2): strong base → E2; otherwise → SN1/E1.
- **Other issues:** E2 is anti-periplanar; Zaitsev and Hofmann rules. SN2 inverts a chiral centre. SN1 racemizes (the
  carbocation is planar). Increased branching on the α and β carbons favors E2 over SN2. A better nucleophile favors
  SN2 over E2; a better base favors E2 over SN2. Carbocations can rearrange. High temperature favors elimination.
- "This flow chart is meant as a guide; there will be exceptions to what is written."
