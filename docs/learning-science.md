# Practise the exam's own task, from memory, on more than one day

The best-supported way to prepare for a hand-written organic chemistry exam that is days away is to spend most of the time producing answers with the notes closed, in the form the exam uses, and to do it again on a later day. Practice testing beats restudy by about half a standard deviation in classrooms (g = 0.50 across 222 studies, [Yang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309), checked). One correct recall on each of three separate days left 68% retained a week later, against 26% for three correct recalls in one sitting ([Rawson & Dunlosky 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484), checked through one secondary source). A new kind of problem should be met first as a worked example that fades into solo work (g = 0.48, [Barbieri et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1.pdf), checked). After that, question types should arrive mixed, because the exam does not say which mechanism applies (g = 0.42 overall, [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/), checked). Feedback has to explain (0.49 for an explanation against 0.05 for right or wrong alone, [Van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://people.acer.org/en/publications/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-s-2/), checked). Rereading, highlighting, points and leaderboards have no comparable support.

Three limits apply throughout, and the last section spells them out. Almost none of these trials are in organic chemistry. The numbers were read from abstracts and secondary summaries, not full texts. Effect sizes (d and g: 0.2 is small by convention, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) depend on what the comparison group did and cannot be added together.

**How to read the citations.** "checked" means a web search in November 2026 confirmed the authors, the year and the number quoted, from an abstract or a summary. Fourteen citations carry that mark. "draft" means: from the research draft, not re-checked. Treat a draft number as a pointer to the paper, not as a fact to quote. Links on draft citations are DOIs taken from the draft and were not opened.

## Retrieval practice

Retrieval practice is producing an answer from memory: drawing a mechanism on a blank page, drawing both chairs, ranking four acids and saying why. Reading a solution is not retrieval.

- **Evidence.** Testing beats restudy by about g = 0.50 across 61 laboratory studies ([Rowland 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/), paper checked; the 0.50 was confirmed only through a later summary that reports it as d). In classrooms the average is g = 0.50 across 222 studies and 48,478 students ([Yang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309), checked). It carries over to new questions: d = 0.40 across 192 transfer effects ([Pan & Rickard 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151), checked through secondary sources). Fact quizzes did not raise scores on higher-order questions, while higher-order or mixed quizzes did ([Agarwal 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000282), draft).
- **Limits.** Transfer was unreliable unless practice matched the final response format, came with explanatory feedback, or went well (Pan & Rickard 2018, draft for this detail). For novices learning a procedure, a test straight after a worked example did not beat studying a second example ([van Gog & Kester 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12002), draft, 40 students). In general chemistry, pre-exam quizzing helped weaker readers, but unsupported "why" items hurt them ([Pyburn et al. 2014](https://scholars.unh.edu/chemistry_facpub/73), draft). The draft found no trial of retrieval practice on mechanism drawing.

## Spacing, successive relearning, and an exam that is days away

Spacing spreads practice of one skill over separate sessions. Successive relearning adds a rule: practise until one correct recall, then do the same again on later days.

- **Evidence.** Spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval by g = 0.74 ([Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8), checked; 39 effect sizes in the abstract, mostly verbal material). The shape of the schedule does not matter: expanding and equal gaps differ by g = 0.03, not significant, over 54 comparisons (same paper, checked). The 68% against 26% result above compared three weekly sessions with one ([Rawson & Dunlosky 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484), checked through one secondary source). On a high-stakes biopsychology exam, successively relearned content scored at least 10 percentage points higher, d = 0.54 to 1.10 ([Janes et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3699), draft).
- **When the exam is days away.** With two study sessions and one test, the best gap was about 20 to 40% of a one-week delay ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/), draft; fact learning). For an exam three to ten days off that suggests gaps of one to three days, with the last successful recall of each skill on the day before.
- **Limits.** Most spacing studies test a week or more later, on facts. For a mathematics procedure tested after one week, massed and spaced practice did about equally well ([Rohrer & Taylor 2006](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1266), draft). The same schedule applied to four probability problem types gave only d = 0.28 ([Rawson, Dunlosky & Janes 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09528-y), from Eureka's equations brief, not re-checked). So on a short runway spacing is a modest gain that costs nothing. Coverage, feedback and practice in the exam's form carry more weight. One correct answer is never proof of mastery.

## Mixing question types

Mixed (interleaved) practice makes the learner decide what kind of problem this is before solving it. Blocked practice (all SN2, then all E2) removes that decision.

- **Evidence.** g = 0.42 across 59 studies, larger when the categories are easy to confuse, and g = 0.34 for mathematics ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/), checked through secondary sources). In a preregistered trial with 54 seventh-grade classes and 787 students, mixed assignments gave 61% against 38% on an unannounced test a month later, d = 0.83 ([Rohrer et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367), checked). College students learned to classify organic compound classes better when examples were mixed ([Eglington & Kang 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2017.07.005), draft). Mixed problem sets beat topic-sorted sets in general chemistry ([Gulacar et al. 2022](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2022/rp/d1rp00334h), draft).
- **Limits.** Mixing hurt for word lists (g = −0.39, Brunmair & Richter, checked) and did not help with expository text. Each procedure has to be workable on its own before mixing helps. The school trial is an upper range, in one US district. Mixing feels worse: physics students who gained most from it rated it harder and believed they had learned less ([Samani & Pan 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-021-00110-x), draft).

## Worked examples that fade

A worked example shows a full solution with the reason for each step. Fading removes steps, the last one first, until the learner solves alone.

- **Evidence.** Worked examples improve mathematics performance by g = 0.48 ([Barbieri et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1.pdf), checked). Correct examples alone did better than incorrect or mixed sets (same paper, checked). Fading gave fewer errors during learning than example and problem pairs ([Renkl et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220970209599510), draft). In web-based general chemistry, examples with self-explanation prompts improved performance and self-efficacy ([Crippen & Earl 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2005.11.018), draft).
- **Limits.** Guidance that helps a novice stops helping, or hurts, as knowledge grows ([Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4), draft). An example should be dropped as soon as a cold attempt succeeds. The draft found no experiment on faded examples for organic mechanisms. Hints need guarding: in one tutor study students abused hints on 36% of actions ([LearnLab](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse), from Eureka's equations brief, not re-checked).

## Self-explanation

Self-explanation is saying in your own words why each step is valid.

- **Evidence.** Prompting it gives g = 0.55 across 69 effects from 64 reports ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x), checked). Organic chemistry students often draw arrows that reach the product without a causal reason ([Bhattacharyya & Bodner 2005](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed082p1402); [Grove et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed2003934); both draft). This exam asks for the reason in sentences, so the reason has to be practised as its own output.
- **Limits.** In the worked-example meta-analysis, adding self-explanation prompts lowered the benefit of the examples (Barbieri et al. 2023, checked). Prompts cost time, and explaining needs some prior knowledge (Pyburn et al. 2014, draft). One short prompt on the step that carries the reasoning, followed by a model answer, fits both results.

## Attempting before being shown

- **Evidence.** A guess before the material is taught helps the content the question asked about (g = 0.54, 97 effects) and almost nothing else in the lesson ([St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8), checked). A longer attempt at a problem before instruction helps conceptual understanding by g = 0.36 ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105), draft).
- **Limits.** With complex material, explicit instruction first matched or beat problem solving first ([Ashman, Kalyuga & Sweller 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09500-5), draft). A multi-step mechanism is complex for most students. The low-risk version is a guess of under a minute, followed at once by the explanation.

## Feedback that explains

- **Evidence.** In computer-based learning, an explanation gives 0.49, the correct answer alone 0.32, and right or wrong alone 0.05, from 70 effects in 40 studies, with the largest gap on higher-order outcomes ([Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen 2015](https://people.acer.org/en/publications/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-s-2/), checked). Explanation feedback beat correct-answer feedback on new transfer questions ([Butler, Godbole & Marsh 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031026), draft). Errors made with high confidence are the ones most likely to be fixed by feedback ([Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491), draft).
- **Limits.** Timing is unresolved. Some laboratory and course studies favour delayed feedback, and the computer-based meta-analysis found smaller effects when feedback was delayed (checked). The guide gives feedback at once after a committed answer and brings a missed item back later, which sidesteps the question without settling it.

## Practising in the exam's own form: by hand, timed, closed book

- **Evidence.** Classroom quizzing effects are larger when the quiz and the exam share a format (Yang et al. 2021, moderator confirmed in the search summary). A matching response format is one of the three conditions for transfer in Pan & Rickard 2018 (draft). Short-answer practice with feedback beat multiple-choice practice whatever the final format ([Kang et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/09541440601056620), draft).
- **Limits.** Closed-book and open-book practice tests gave similar retention in one laboratory study ([Agarwal et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1391), draft). The closed-book rule rests mainly on the exam being closed book and on honest self-assessment. The draft found no direct study of timed practice, of writing in pen, or of choosing seven questions from nine. Those recommendations are inferences from format matching and arithmetic.

## Drawing and building models for spatial ideas

- **Evidence.** Drawing your own picture helps against reading alone, but results are mixed against studying a good provided picture ([Fiorella & Zhang 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9444-8), draft). Organic chemistry students who used a model kit translated between wedge, Newman and Fischer drawings more accurately, yet most did not pick the kit up unless prompted ([Stull et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2012.719956), draft). Virtual models worked about as well as physical ones ([Stull & Hegarty 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000077), draft).
- **Limits.** The case for drawing by hand here is that the exam demands it, not that drawing is a stronger way to learn. The draft found no study comparing hand drawing with on-screen structure editors for later exam scores. Every number on models is unchecked.

## Knowing what you know

- **Evidence.** Judgements of learning made after a delay are far more accurate than those made right after study, g = 0.93 for relative accuracy ([Rhodes & Tauber 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021705), checked). In a survey of 177 undergraduates most reread and few self-tested ([Karpicke, Butler & Roediger 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19358016/), from Eureka's equations brief, not re-checked). In general chemistry, lower performers overestimated their exam scores ([Pazicni & Bauer 2014](https://doi.org/10.1039/C3RP00106G), draft). Scoring your own recall against a list of required ideas reduces overconfidence ([Dunlosky et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2010.502239), draft).
- **Limits.** Most of this research uses definitions and texts. Nothing was found on how accurately students mark their own hand-drawn mechanisms, which the written questions in this guide depend on.

## Sleep and cramming

- **Evidence.** In an MIT introductory chemistry class (100 students given activity trackers, 88 completing), sleep duration, quality and consistency over the preceding weeks accounted for nearly 25% of the variance in quiz and midterm scores. Sleep on the single night before a test was unrelated to the score ([Okano et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6773696), checked). Massed rereading helps on an immediate test and fades fast ([Rawson & Kintsch 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.97.1.70), draft).
- **Limits.** The sleep result is a correlation in one class. It suggests that one good night cannot repair weeks of short sleep, and equally that a late night of cramming buys little. It does not show that sleeping more would raise a given student's grade.

## What does not help

- **Rereading and highlighting.** Both were rated low utility in a review of ten techniques, where practice testing and distributed practice were the only two rated high ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266), draft; also cited in Eureka's equations brief). Rereading feels productive because the page becomes familiar, and familiarity is not the ability to produce the answer.
- **Points, badges and leaderboards.** In a semester-long comparison of two sections of one course (71 students), the section with a leaderboard and badges ended with lower motivation and lower final exam scores ([Hanus & Fox 2015](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c408t-d82df39d2133f93d47d3d98f9c9aadee3039f9f89021367e6820fad79509cf9e3/Details?lng=en), checked). It is one small study and does not show that every game element harms learning. The average effect of gamification on learning is positive but unstable ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w), draft). This class adds neither.

## What the guide does about each

| Technique | What this guide does | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice | Every question needs a committed answer before any solution shows. A viewed solution does not count as practice. | Practice (`drill/`), the question-type trainers in Exam 2 Lab (`exam/`), the check on Foundations |
| Successive relearning | A missed question comes back in the same session after up to three others, at most twice, and only while another question is left to stand between the tries: straight after its worked solution a try would test nothing. Once answered right it stops coming back, and returns on later days. One still missed is due again at once. | Practice sessions; the list of misses on Review (`review/`) |
| Spacing sized to the exam | After a correct answer the gap grows through 1, 2, 4, 7 and 14 days, and is cut so that at least two more reviews fit before the exam date, when one is set. A right answer within 12 hours of the last answer to the same question does not grow the gap: after a miss the solution was just read, and a question answered again minutes later is massed practice, not spacing. The growing shape is a convenience: the evidence says total spacing matters, not the shape. | The scheduler in `assets/core.js`; the exam date is set on the home page |
| Mastery measured after a delay | A question type counts as "retained" only when it is answered right again a day or more later: on a later calendar day than the last answer, and at least six hours after it. The first number shown is delayed accuracy, not same-day accuracy. | The mastery levels shown per question type |
| Mixing | Once a type has been met, its questions arrive mixed with others, ordered so that no two neighbours share a type. | Practice, "mixed" mode (the default) |
| Worked examples that fade | Each question type has worked examples revealed one step at a time. Mechanisms can be watched, then completed with some arrows missing, then drawn alone. Inside a question the trainer only asks for the arrows: drawn on screen, or on paper first. | The type pages in Exam 2 Lab; Mechanism Studio (`mechanisms/`) |
| Self-explanation | Two to four steps of each worked example ask a question to answer before reading on. Each section of a reading has a "why" question. | Worked examples; Readings (`read/`) |
| Attempting first | The same before-you-read-on questions, and the Foundations check, which is taken before the refreshers. A hint in the mechanism trainer is offered only after an attempt, and a mechanism finished on the last hint or with "Show me" counts as a miss, earns no XP and comes back. | Worked examples; Foundations (`foundations/`); Mechanism Studio |
| Explanatory feedback | Every choice has its own reason, shown for the answer picked. Then the worked solution, step by step, and the one-line rule. | Every question |
| The exam's own form | Written questions are done on paper with a suggested time, then marked line by line against a rubric beside the model answer. Mechanisms have a "paper" mode: draw first, then reveal and check. Mock exams follow the real exam's format. | Written questions; Mechanism Studio; Mock exams (`mock/`) |
| Spatial ideas | Chairs can be built and flipped, Newman projections turned, and 3D models rotated. The guide does not prompt for the physical model kit on each question. Use it anyway: it is allowed in the exam. | Molecule Lab (`lab/`); chair questions |
| Knowing what you know | Confidence (guess, unsure, sure) is asked before feedback on written and mechanism work, so that confident errors stand out. Misses and flagged questions are collected. | Written and mechanism questions; Review |
| Sleep | Nothing is enforced. The plan below builds it in. | This document |
| No rereading as the main activity, no points, no leaderboards | Readings are short and end in questions. This class adds no points, streak penalties or rankings of its own. | Readings; everywhere |

## How to use the guide

These plans are built from the findings above. They have not been tested as plans. Assume two to two and a half hours a day in two blocks.

**Seven days before the exam**

| Days out | Main job |
|---|---|
| 7 | Set the exam date on the home page. Take the Foundations check and read the refreshers for what you missed. Do half a mock exam on paper to find the weak question types. |
| 6 | The two weakest types: the worked examples, then cold questions. Finish with a mixed Practice session. |
| 5 | The next two types, the same way. Clear what is due in Practice first. |
| 4 | A full mock exam: on paper, in pen, timed, notes closed, model kit on the desk. Mark it against the rubrics the same day. |
| 3 | Repair what the mock showed. Mixed Practice. Draw each missed mechanism from blank until one clean version. |
| 2 | A second full mock in the morning. Repair in the afternoon. Stop in the evening. |
| 1 | Nothing new. One cold answer for each question type, the Review list, a normal bedtime. |

Every day starts with what is due in Practice, done cold, before anything is reread.

**Three days before the exam.** Day 3: the Foundations check for anything shaky, half a mock, then worked examples for the two weakest types. Day 2: one full timed mock on paper in the morning, repair and mixed Practice in the afternoon. Day 1: as above, nothing new. With this little time, skip the long readings unless a question type makes no sense without them, and go to its type page in Exam 2 Lab instead.

**The last 48 hours**

| Window | Do | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| 48 to 36 hours | The last full timed mock, marked straight away | Starting topics never studied |
| 36 to 24 hours | Repair the two or three weakest types with worked examples, then cold questions. A short evening session. A full night of sleep. | Rereading notes as the main activity |
| 24 to 12 hours | Two blocks of 40 to 50 minutes: one cold answer per question type, and the Review list down to one clean answer each | Another full mock, marathon sessions |
| Last evening | Ten to fifteen minutes of quick facts (pKa values, A-values, solvent classes), then stop | Late-night cramming |
| Exam morning | A ten-minute warm-up: a chair flip, an SN2 with its arrows, an acidity ranking | New problems |

The reasoning: with a day or two left the useful gap between two attempts is hours to a day, every skill should get one last successful recall, nothing new should go in that cannot be recalled twice before the exam, and the marginal hour of sleep is probably worth more than the marginal hour of rereading.

## Limits

- **Checking was partial.** Fourteen citations were searched for and are marked "checked". For each, the authors and year were confirmed, and the number was confirmed from an abstract or a secondary summary, never from the full text. For Rowland 2014, Pan & Rickard 2018, Brunmair & Richter 2019 and the 68% against 26% result, the number was found only in secondary sources. The search corrected two details of the draft: Latimier et al. report 39 effect sizes for the spacing comparison (the draft said 29 studies), and Hanus & Fox had 71 students (the draft said 80). Other draft details may be off in the same way.
- **Everything marked "draft" is unverified.** The draft says that many of its citations, including several DOIs, were written from memory. Open the paper before quoting any of them or using them to justify a change in another course.
- **Almost none of it is organic chemistry.** The effect sizes come from word pairs, texts, school mathematics and introductory psychology. The chemistry sources are mostly general chemistry, small, or qualitative. Applying these numbers to mechanism drawing and chair conformations is an assumption.
- **The short runway is under-studied.** Most spacing and relearning studies test after a week or more. The plans above extrapolate from a ratio measured with two study sessions and fact learning.
- **Retrieval practice for procedures is contested.** Some experiments found no benefit of testing over example study for novices. The order used here (example, fade, then cold practice) is a reasoned compromise, not a tested package.
- **Self-marking is a weak point.** Written questions depend on students marking their own drawings against a rubric. The evidence that rubrics improve self-marking comes from definitions, not structures.
- **Exam tactics have no direct evidence.** Nothing was found on choosing among questions, on writing in pen, or on time budgets.
- **The guide itself is untested.** No student data exist for it yet. Its own records (delayed accuracy by question type, confident errors) are the best available test of whether these choices work here.
- **Left out on purpose.** The draft also covers page design principles, test anxiety and motivation theory. None of those citations were confirmed, so they are not used here.
